# The lint rule

At a company I joined in October 2019, a concession on one lint rule — a style preference granted
to one team because it was cheaper than the argument — became the boundary case that split a
division.

## The rule was never about the rule

A shared lint config is the cheapest federalism test a company ever runs: one file, every team's
code, no product surface to hide behind. If it cannot survive one concession, the schema will not
survive one either.

The split did not happen because of a semicolon. It happened because nobody could say where the
rule stopped being style and started being a contract, so the next argument was about who owned
the seam. Two teams left with two conventions, then two roadmaps, then two org charts.

## What it cost

A VP told a room of engineers it was fine, because I was going to fix it. I handed in my notice
that week and was gone by June 2021. I was not going to be the person who absorbed the cost of an
undecided boundary and called it a fix.

I was granted $33 on 14 October 2019. At the last quote I checked the share price was $2 — a
sixteenth of the grant, and closer to a fiftieth with the option stack counted honestly. The market
did not punish a lint rule. It priced a company that could not decide who owned a seam.

## The general case

This is [checkable claims](/ideas/checkable-claims) at organisational scale. A contract that is
enforced by convention is a claim with no receipt: it holds while everyone happens to agree, and
the moment someone does not, there is no artifact to point at. The cost is not the argument. The
cost is the undecided boundary, paid later by everyone who has to work across it.

The same seam shows up in the [federated graph work](/about/disciplines): a service declares what
it claims and what it presupposes, addressed so drift in the presupposed surface breaks the build
instead of the customer. That is the lint rule with the boundary written down before the argument.
